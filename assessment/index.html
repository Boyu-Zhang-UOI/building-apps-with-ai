Assessment ​

How your work is graded, and why. The question is never "did it run?" on its own. It is "did you specify it, check it, secure it, and can you explain it?"

This page covers grading for Building Apps with AI (CS [2xxx], University of Idaho): 3 credits over a 16-week semester. A credit means about 45 hours of work (catalog Regulation D-1), so plan on about 135 hours, or about 8.5 hours a week: two 75-minute studios (Studio A — Learn & build and Studio B — Workshop) plus about 6 hours outside class. Weeks 1–15 are teaching weeks; week 16 is finals week, when students present their capstones in the course's scheduled final exam period (Regulation H-1-b). There is no final examination.

It is for students and instructors. The detailed scoring guides are in Rubrics and the before-and-after course survey in Self-Assessment: Before and After; the weekly AI-free exit-ticket questions are for instructors. Assignments are submitted on Canvas, the course's learning management system (LMS).

These match the syllabus.

Where the home page and Project 2 fit. The home page (weeks 1–4) counts inside Assignments, through the weekly homework. Project 2, the AI micro-app, is the homework for weeks 9 and 10, so those two weeks get no separate homework score. The capstone pitch, due in week 10, counts in Assignments.

Grading philosophy ​

Process and explanation, not just a running program. AI makes running code cheap. It doesn't make understanding cheap: in Anthropic's randomized trial, junior engineers who learned a new library with AI help scored 50% on a follow-up quiz, against 67% for those without it, and fell furthest behind on debugging (Anthropic). Students who struggled with AI tools finished believing they had done better than they had (Prather et al.). Newer studies point the same way. Students who had to explain AI-written code before adding it failed an AI-off maintenance task far less often than students who didn't (39% versus 77%; Sankaranarayanan), and AI users remembered less of their own work two days later (Bergh et al.). So most of your grade rests on things that show understanding and good habits:

  • Evidence of process: your spec, tests, security checklist with evidence, PROMPTS.md (including short "teach-back" notes that explain each AI-written change) and git history.
  • Explanation with the AI off: the weekly exit tickets and the AI-off lab tasks, where you practice the six skills you must show without AI, and the questions about your code after your final presentation.
  • Your own writing: reflections and peer reviews.

This is where the field has gone. A 2026 study of 23 US courses that require AI in graded software work found that all 14 conventionally graded courses counted participation (median weight 17.5%), projects had a median weight of 50%, and only three had any proctored assessment (Geng et al., arXiv 2608.05898). The ACM Task Force on generative AI and programming assessment surveyed about 500 educators in 2026: 68% had already changed their assessment, most often toward in-person checks, oral defenses and grading process over product (ACM Task Force report).

No arms race. AI is required for most of the work in this course, so we don't try to make assignments AI-proof, and we don't use AI-detection software. Instead:

  1. Weekly labs are graded on effort and completion. It's safe to experiment, fail and document what went wrong.
  2. Quality is judged at project milestones, against rubrics that reward specs, edge cases, security evidence and honest documentation.
  3. Understanding is checked with the AI off: in the weekly exit tickets, in AI-off lab tasks such as the Debug Clinic (week 7) and the AI-off maintenance task (week 14), and in the questions after your final presentation. In the agent weeks you also write a teach-back for every agent commit, because that's when understanding drops: in CMU 15-113's survey data, students' self-rated understanding fell to its lowest point of the year once agents took over (Student Wisdom report).

A working demo is required but not sufficient. AI makes brittle prototypes easy. Courses like Utah's CS 3960 give credit only for edge cases, how features interact and taste (syllabus), and Michigan's build rubric is half spec and design, half implementation and tests (EECS 498). Our capstone rubric works the same way.

How grades are calculated ​

Each component is scored in points, then scaled to its weight: points earned ÷ points possible × weight.

ComponentWhat is scoredPoints possibleDrops
Assignments (20%)Weekly homework for weeks 1–8 and 11–15 at 10 points each (weekly rubric); weekly reflections for weeks 1–9 and 11–14 at 5 points each; two peer reviews (weeks 5 and 14) at 5 points each; the final reflection (week 15) at 5 points; the capstone pitch (week 10) at 5 points185 (after the drops)Your lowest two of the thirteen homework weeks and your lowest two weekly reflections are dropped (never the peer reviews, the final reflection or the pitch)
Labs (20%)30 studios (weeks 1–15) at 10 points each, including the exit tickets260 (after the drops)Your lowest four studios are dropped
Project 1 (15%)Project 1 rubric40—
Project 2 (15%)Project 2 rubric20—
Capstone (30%)Capstone rubric, including the final presentation100—

Example: 148 of 185 assignment points is 148 ÷ 185 × 20 = 16% of the course.

The stretch tier of homework is optional and never affects your grade.

How to submit ​

  • Projects: on Canvas, submit the repo URL, the live URL (from week 10 on, the production URL plus a screenshot of it working, because the host deletes old preview links), and the link to your final commit. Step by step: Projects: How to submit.
  • Weekly homework: commit to the relevant repo, tick the deliverables checklist in that week's homework.md, and submit the links on Canvas.
  • Reflections, peer reviews and other personal writing: on Canvas only, never in a repository (Privacy of your work).
  • Exit tickets: weeks 1–14, at the end of each week's Studio B (or of Studio A, when a timed activity fills Studio B), on Canvas with AI tools closed (or on paper). Weeks 15 and 16 have none: Regulation H-1-a allows no quizzes or exams in class in the week before finals week.

Due dates. Unless Canvas says otherwise, everything is due before next week's Studio A (default 11:59 pm the night before). The capstone pitch is due 72 hours before week 11 Studio A. The capstone (production URL and screenshot, repo URL, link to the final commit) and the final reflection with your revised AI-use rules (week 15 homework) are due at the start of the course's final exam period in finals week, when you present. This always falls before the University's grade deadline.

We grade the commit you submitted, as it was at the deadline.

Privacy of your work ​

Your projects are public on purpose: they become your portfolio. Your grades and your personal writing are not. U of I policy treats grades and graded work as private (FSH 4700 F-6, and FSH 2600, which applies FERPA, the US federal student-records law), so the course keeps the two apart.

In your public repos (under your GitHub username)On Canvas only
Code; SPEC.md, TESTS.md, PROMPTS.md, AGENTS.md, SECURITY_CHECKLIST.md, README.md; supabase/policies.sql; screenshots of your app with made-up dataWeekly and final reflections (including the week 6 bake-off verdict); exit tickets; your personal AI-use rules; the signed safety contract; self-assessments; written peer reviews; a pair's signed contribution statement; the capstone pitch; your project submissions (links and screenshots)
  • Never commit grades, feedback, reflections, the safety contract, classmates' real names (refer to people by GitHub username), your student ID, or real personal data about anyone (project rules).
  • You don't need your real name anywhere public. A GitHub username that doesn't reveal your name is fine, and so is leaving out a photo.
  • Code review on GitHub is fine. Pull-request comments about code are ordinary code review. Keep them about the code. The graded peer review is the written one on Canvas.
  • Private repos are allowed. Add your instructor as a collaborator. Check Tool Handout first, because some free hosting needs a public repo.
  • Grades and feedback come back on Canvas only. Staff never post feedback as GitHub issues, pull-request comments or commits on your repos.
  • Recordings: you may record a studio only with the instructor's consent or as an approved CDAR accommodation, and in either case must tell the instructor first (FSH 4700 F-8).
  • Class results (for example from the self-assessment) are shared only as summaries in which no one can be identified.
  • After the course your repos are yours: you can keep them public, make them private or delete them.

Late work and grace tokens ​

  • Two grace tokens. Each token extends one deadline by 48 hours, no reason needed. Write "grace token" in your Canvas submission comment. You don't need to ask first. (A token on the pitch is allowed, but the instructor's reply may then come at the start of week 11 Studio A.)
  • Tokens can't be used for live events: studios, exit tickets and the final presentations. Those have make-up rules instead (below).
  • Pairs: a pair can use a token on the capstone only if both partners have one left, and it costs each partner one.
  • After your tokens run out: late work loses 10% of that item's points per day, for up to 3 days. After that it scores zero unless you've agreed an extension.
  • Tool trouble is not by itself a reason to be late, because switching to a fallback is part of the course (I ran out of free credits). If every fallback fails, take screenshots of the errors and contact your instructor before the deadline; you won't be penalized.

Absences and make-up work ​

Attendance isn't scored on its own. It affects your grade through Labs (20%): each studio is scored out of 10 for the work you do in it, and a studio missed without arrangement or make-up scores 0. Your lowest four are dropped, which covers a few ordinary missed days.

  • Absences the University recognizes (Regulation M-1): official university activities, your own illness, family illness or care, or other compelling circumstances. Tell your instructor as soon as you can (before the absence, for official activities). You can make up the studio for full credit within one week: do the lab's core parts and, for the studio with that week's exit ticket, the exit ticket on Canvas. Deadlines move without using a token. A final presentation missed for one of these reasons gets a make-up that your instructor arranges.
  • Religious observance (Regulation M-3): exit tickets and the final presentations are rescheduled. Tell your instructor as early as you can.
  • Other absences: do the lab and (for the studio with the exit ticket) the exit ticket within 48 hours for up to 7 of the 10 points.
  • The grade deadline is firm. Fix dates and resubmissions must all fall before the University's final-grade deadline. If extenuating circumstances make that impossible, an Incomplete may be agreed (Regulation F).

Working in pairs ​

Pairs are allowed on the capstone only. The full rules are in Capstone (weeks 11–16): Working solo or in a pair. In short:

  • Pairs take on a bigger scope: at least three of the four feature types, or two plus a scope increase the instructor approves at the pitch.
  • Each partner commits their own work under their own GitHub account, and work is merged through pull requests reviewed by the other partner.
  • The README has a Contributions table that uses GitHub usernames. The signed contribution statement goes on Canvas.
  • Reflections, peer reviews, exit tickets and the self-assessment are always individual, and in the final presentation each partner answers questions about the code.
  • Both partners normally get the same capstone score. If the history and the contribution statement show a big imbalance, the instructor talks with both partners and may adjust individual scores.

Pass/fail and non-credit versions ​

This course is letter-graded (A–F) for everyone.

The student pass/fail option (Regulation B-10). You may be able to take the course under the University's pass/fail option. Eligibility and limits are set by the regulation, so check with your advisor. Instructors aren't told who chose it: the instructor reports a letter grade, and the Registrar records a C or higher as P. For undergraduates, a D stays on the record as a D.

Not applicable to U of I credit: the two versions below are for people using the course materials elsewhere.

  • Non-credit (workshops, bootcamps, community classes). No grades. A participant completes the course with a live home page, Project 1, Project 2 and a capstone, each with its documents; a final presentation to the group or a mentor, with questions about the code; and at least half the studios attended, or the labs done self-paced.
  • Self-paced learners. Follow the self-paced path. Use the rubrics as checklists. Answer each week's Understanding check with the AI off before looking anything up (the exit tickets themselves are kept for instructors). After each project, record yourself explaining at least one function for three minutes with the AI off (the format UCSD's CS1-LLM used for its project videos, Vadaparty et al.). Take the self-assessment at the start and the end.

AI use policy ​

This course requires AI. The rule is not "how much AI did you use" but "can you explain what you shipped". Every task is labeled with one of three levels, always in words as well as color. This is the complete list of 🟡 AI limited and 🔴 AI off work, and it is the one that counts; the syllabus summarizes it under AI Use.

LevelWhenThe complete list
🟢 AI onBuilding, explaining, learningGenerating code; asking for explanations, plans and reviews of your diffs; debugging outside the Debug Clinic; translating instructions into your own language. Everything not listed below is 🟢 AI on
🟡 AI limitedWhere the goal is your own thinkingYour SPEC.md (Project 1 and capstone): the AI may ask questions, critique it and run the Goldfish test, but you write it. Your capstone pitch: critique only. The Debug Clinic (week 7): AI off, then hint-only tutor mode after 10 minutes stuck on one bug. "You write it" (week 8): the AI may plan and review, but you write the code. Any other task a brief marks 🟡 AI limited follows that brief
🔴 AI offChecks of your understanding, and writing addressed to peopleExit tickets · "explain it" conversations and the questions after your final presentation · weekly and final reflections, including the week 6 bake-off Verdict · written peer reviews (an AI review may come after yours, to compare) · peer explanation swaps · the pre- and post-course self-assessments · your personal AI-use rules (week 2, revised in week 15) · your response to the AI fluency pre-work · the first draft of your Project 1 spec (week 5) · the hand-made code changes and the feature you write by hand in week 1 · the prompt-makeover rewrites (week 4) · Project 2's "Where the key lives and who can read it" section (week 10) · the AI-off maintenance task (week 14) · the teach-back notes and the "What I wrote or decided myself" section of PROMPTS.md · a pair's contribution statement · any other task a brief marks 🔴 AI off

If two labels ever seem to disagree, follow the stricter one and ask. Using AI on 🔴 AI-off work, or beyond what a 🟡 AI-limited label allows, is academic dishonesty under the University's Student Code of Conduct (Academic integrity).

  • Disclose: every project has a PROMPTS.md with your key prompts, links to exported chats where possible, notes on what you changed, and teach-back notes for AI-written changes. Commits made with AI help end with an Assisted-by: [tool] line (taught in week 3, required from week 11).
  • Own it: "If you can't explain it, you didn't build it." You may be asked to explain any line you submit.
  • Credit others: if you use code, images or text from a person or website, credit it in your README. Using prompts you found online is fine; say where they came from.
  • Your own rules: in week 2 you write a few personal rules for when you will and won't use AI, and in week 15 you revise them. Students who set their own rules tend to do better (Azimi).

Academic integrity ​

Disclosure is the rule. Your PROMPTS.md is where you show how you used AI. Honest disclosure is never penalized, even when the AI did most of the typing.

What counts as academic dishonesty here. The U of I Student Code of Conduct and Resolution Process (FSH 2300, section E-1) covers it. In this course that includes:

  • using AI on 🔴 AI-off work, or beyond what a 🟡 AI-limited label allows ("reliance on prohibited technological assistance/artificial intelligence tools", FSH 2300 E-1.c);
  • fabricating evidence: test results you didn't run, security evidence you didn't collect, or PROMPTS.md entries that didn't happen (AI agents have faked test results; we hold ourselves to a higher standard);
  • submitting someone else's code, prompts or writing as your own, including a past student's project or prompts without credit;
  • submitting a project made for another course without the prior approval of both instructors;
  • having someone else take your exit ticket.

There are two separate processes, and they never mix.

1. "Explain it or redo it": a learning conversation, not a sanction. Sometimes you can't yet explain part of your own work, or PROMPTS.md doesn't seem to match what's in the repo. Then your instructor asks for a short conversation, about 10 minutes, with the AI off. Nobody is alleging misconduct. Usually you can explain it and that's the end of it. If you can't yet, you redo that part within 7 days: study or rebuild it, update PROMPTS.md, and explain it in a short follow-up. The part is then graded on the normal rubric, with no cap and no penalty for needing the follow-up. If, during a conversation, your instructor comes to suspect misconduct, they will stop, tell you so, and use the University process below instead.

2. Suspected academic dishonesty: the University's process. If your instructor suspects academic dishonesty, they will tell you and notify the Dean of Students, as FSH 2300 F-9.b.1 requires. No grade consequence is applied until that process has finished and its decision has been communicated to you (F-9.b.10). If grades are due before then, an Incomplete is entered until it's resolved (F-9.b.11). The Dean of Students explains your options and rights, including an informal resolution if everyone agrees to one.

We don't use AI detectors. Their verdicts can't be checked or explained. A conversation about your work tells us what we actually need to know: whether you understand it.

Accommodations ​

If you believe you need disability-related academic adjustments, contact the Center for Disability Access and Resources (CDAR). We follow CDAR accommodation letters. Also tell your instructor in week 1 if a health condition, time zone, device or connectivity issue could affect your work, so you can plan together. Many adjustments are open to everyone, with or without a letter:

  • Exit tickets: extra time, typed answers, large print or screen-reader-friendly forms.
  • Timed builds: may be untimed; they're graded on effort and save points, not speed.
  • Final presentation: a pre-recorded demo with live questions, or a written script with screenshots, if presenting live is a barrier. The questions afterwards can be answered in writing or with extra time. The same options apply to an "explain it or redo it" conversation.
  • Deadlines: extensions as set out in your accommodation letter, on top of your grace tokens.
  • Tools: a guide to the course's tools with a screen reader or keyboard. Codespaces runs in a browser on modest laptops and Chromebooks. If your connection is unreliable, talk to your instructor about campus machines or offline alternatives.

Feedback and regrades ​

  • Grades and written feedback come back on Canvas, aiming for within a week and before the next related deadline.
  • Regrade requests: within 7 days of getting a grade, in writing on Canvas, naming the rubric row and your reason. The whole item may be regraded, so the score can go up or down.
