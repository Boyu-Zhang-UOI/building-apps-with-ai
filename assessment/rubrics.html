Rubrics ​

Scoring guides for every graded component. Each row is something a grader can see in your repo, on your live site or in your writing.

How to read these tables. Each rubric has criteria (rows) and four levels (columns). The number in bold is the points for that level.

LevelMeans
ExemplaryEverything asked for, done carefully, with clear evidence. Beyond what most students do
ProficientEverything asked for, done well. This is a good result
DevelopingStarted, but important parts are missing or unclear
MissingNot there

Graders pick the level that best fits each row. If a row falls between two levels, they use the lower one and say what would lift it. Weights and how points turn into your course grade are in the assessment README.

Rubric levels describe the work, not misconduct. If a piece of work seems to break the AI-use rules (for example, an AI-written SPEC.md or reflection) or seems fabricated, it is handled under Academic integrity, and no score is changed until the University process is complete.

The six skills you must show without AI (syllabus) aren't scored in these rubrics. They are a requirement of their own: each skill must be shown at least once in an oral walkthrough, a retake or a short follow-up, and showing all six is needed for a C or higher (letter grades). The weekly exit tickets are where you practice them.

Weekly labs and homework ​

20% of the course. Graded on effort and completion of the core tier, not on polish. The core items for each week are the deliverables checklist in that week's homework.md (each week's folder is linked from the schedule). For week 12, the core items are the capstone's Milestone 1 row, copied word for word. Milestone 2 is spread over weeks 13 and 14: week 13's core items are its second feature type and section 3 of the security checklist, and week 14's are the rest of the Milestone 2 row.

Weeks 1–8 and 11–15 are each scored out of 10, and the lowest two are dropped. Weeks 9 and 10 are scored once, together, with the AI micro-app rubric (20 points, which include the capstone pitch); they get no separate weekly scores. Week 16 (finals week) is graded in the capstone.

Some weekly deliverables are also parts of a project (for example, the week 1 page is the start of your home page). That's deliberate: the weekly score rewards doing the step on time, and the project rubric judges the finished result.

CriterionExemplaryProficientDevelopingMissing
Core tasks4 · Every core item on the checklist is done3 · All but one core item done2 · All but two done; 1 · about half done0 · Not submitted
Save points and log3 · Several small commits with messages that say what changed; PROMPTS.md updated with what you changed or checked yourself; AI-assisted commits carry an Assisted-by: line (from week 3)2 · Commits with clear messages; PROMPTS.md updated; from week 11, Assisted-by: lines on AI-assisted commits1 · One large commit, or PROMPTS.md not updated0 · No commits this week
Checked it, or documented the blocker3 · Wrote the "done when" check before asking for the code (from week 6, an automated test wherever the logic allows it), tested the normal case and at least one edge case, and says what happened. If something didn't work: the exact error, what you tried, and what you'll try next2 · Tested the normal case, or described the blocker with the error message1 · "It doesn't work", with no detail0 · Nothing

A lab that didn't work, with a clear record of the error and what you tried, can still earn full marks. That's the point of effort grading.

Stretch tier: optional, and it never changes your score.

In-class work and participation ​

15% of the course (the syllabus calls this "In-class builds, exit tickets and participation"). There are 30 studios, two a week in weeks 1–15: Studio A — Learn & build and Studio B — Workshop. The Project Fair in finals week counts as one more session. Each of the 31 is scored out of 10, and your lowest four are dropped, so 270 points count. Timed builds such as Game in an Hour are graded on effort, not on whether you finish.

CriterionExemplaryProficientDevelopingMissing
Lab work4 · Works through the core parts, reaches the checkpoints or asks for help early, and helps a neighbor at least once3 · Works through the core parts and reaches most checkpoints1 · Present but mostly off-task, or leaves early without arrangement0 · Absent without arrangement
Peer explanation and share-out3 · Explains their own work to a peer (in a swap, show and tell or debrief) and asks a genuine question about the peer's2 · Takes part in the swap or share-out1 · Minimal part (listens only)0 · Doesn't take part
Checkpoint or exit ticket (🔴 AI off for the ticket)3 · The studio with that week's exit ticket (usually Studio B, weeks 1–14): every exit-ticket question answered in your own words. Correctness does not affect this score. Any other studio, including both in week 15: shares the end-of-studio checkpoint (a live URL, screenshot or commit), or the exact blocker2 · All but one exit-ticket question answered; or shares where they got to, without the evidence1 · Only one or two exit-ticket questions answered; or present at the checkpoint but shares nothing0 · Absent; or no exit ticket

The Project Fair (finals week) is scored with the same rows: Lab work is presenting your app at your station; Peer explanation and share-out is visiting classmates' stations and leaving feedback cards; Checkpoint is your app working at its production URL during the Fair.

Missed a studio or the Fair? The make-up rules follow U of I Regulations M-1 and M-3. For a recognized absence (official university activities, your own illness, family illness or care, other compelling circumstances, or religious observance), do the lab's core parts and, for the studio with that week's exit ticket, the exit ticket on Canvas within one week, for full credit; for the Fair, your instructor arranges a make-up. Other absences: within 48 hours, for up to 7 of the 10 points.

No partner? With an odd number of students, or when your partner is away, explain your work to the instructor or a TA instead; that counts as the peer explanation. Online cohorts: "present" means camera or chat participation in the session, and swaps happen in breakout rooms.

Capstone ​

30% of the course, 100 points. For the capstone brief.

IMPORTANT

A working demo is required but not sufficient. Without a production URL where the main user story works, the capstone is capped at 50/100 until it runs. Agree a fix date with your instructor; it must fall before the University grade deadline (otherwise an Incomplete may be agreed under Regulation F). A working demo on its own earns only the "Deployment" row and part of the "Feature types" row. Most points are for the spec, tests, security, process, documentation and code you can explain.

Reflections and peer reviews ​

10% of the course. All of this is 🔴 AI off writing, submitted on Canvas (never in a repository). It's graded on specifics and honesty, not polish or grammar. The component is made of:

PartPointsShare of the component
Weekly reflections (template, 150–300 words) for weeks 1–9 and 11–15, out of 10 each, lowest two dropped. Week 6's is the AI-free Verdict on your bake-off scorecard. Week 10 has none: the micro-app's "Where the key lives" section takes its place and is graded in the micro-app rubric12040%
Two written peer reviews, out of 10 each: the week 5 spec review and the week 14 code review2030%
Final capstone reflection (prompts), with your revised personal AI-use rules2030%

Component score = 10% × (0.4 × reflections ÷ 120 + 0.3 × peer reviews ÷ 20 + 0.3 × final ÷ 20).

Writing that appears to be AI-generated is handled under Academic integrity; no score is changed until the University process is complete.

Peer review (10 points) ​

Both reviews are written on Canvas. Share yours with the author privately (a Canvas comment or email), never as an issue, pull-request comment or file in either repository, and refer to people by GitHub username.

  • Week 5 spec review: after the studio swap, write up your review of a classmate's SPEC.md: your prediction of what the app will do; every criterion that can't be tested (by ID) and why; one missing edge case; one strength; one question for the author.
  • Week 14 code review: use the headings of the CODE_REVIEW template. You may also leave ordinary comments on the author's pull request on GitHub; the graded review is the Canvas one.
  • No classmate available? Your instructor assigns a review of a practice spec or repo instead.
CriterionExemplaryProficientDevelopingMissing
Specific and located4 · Every point names where: an acceptance criterion ID, a file and line, a step on the live site3 · Most points are located1 · Mostly general comments ("looks good")0 · Not submitted
Accurate3 · Claims checked against the real spec or live app; any disagreement is phrased as a question2 · Mostly accurate1 · At least one claim that's clearly wrong (a problem that isn't there)0 · Largely wrong findings
Useful and kind3 · One real strength, the most important fix with a suggestion, and one question for the author2 · A strength and a fix1 · Only praise, or only criticism0 · Unkind or unusable

Invented problems cost points, as they do in UCSD's AI course, which penalizes reviews with made-up errors (UCSD).
