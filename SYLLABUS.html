Building Apps with AI — Syllabus ​

Build real software with AI, on a $0 budget. University of Idaho · CS [2xxx] · 3 credits · a 16-week semester · two 75-minute studios a week plus about 6 hours outside class · prerequisite: one programming course in any language.

1. Course description ​

In 2025 Andrej Karpathy named "vibe coding": describe what you want to an AI, accept what it writes, and keep going on vibes. It's fast, and many vibe-coded apps then leaked their users' data. This course teaches both halves. You build and ship web apps by directing AI tools, from a chat window in week 1 to an agent in your own repository. You also learn the habits that make that software trustworthy: a spec before the prompt, a check before the code, small commits, tests, reading what the AI wrote, and keeping secrets and data safe. You already program, so the course teaches what's new: the web, git, and how to direct an AI and check its work.

2. Who this course is for ​

This course is for students from any major (engineering, data science, business, the sciences, the arts and more) who have taken one programming course in any language, such as CS 1112, CS 1120, CS 2120 or another department's programming course, or have equivalent experience or instructor permission. You can write a loop and a function; you don't need to know HTML, CSS, JavaScript, the terminal, git or how the web works, because the course teaches them, and From Python to JavaScript covers the differences that trip Python programmers up. CS students who have completed CS 3383 Software Engineering should take the upper-division course Engineering Software with AI (CS [4xxx]/[5xxx]) instead, because it builds on what they already know. All you need is a laptop with a modern browser and free accounts (Accounts); the required cost is $0 (Tools and cost).

3. Learning outcomes ​

By the end of the course you will be able to:

  • Direct AI tools to build working web software, from single-file pages to a deployed full-stack app, using a repeatable workflow (the Safe Loop).
  • Specify what you want before you build it: a one-page spec with user stories and testable acceptance criteria.
  • Verify AI output instead of trusting it: write the check first, run it, test the happy path and edge cases, and read the diff.
  • Explain every part of the code you submit, and debug a failing program by reading its error messages, with the AI switched off.
  • Use version control (git and GitHub) as a safety net: commit, compare, revert, branch and review through pull requests.
  • Build safely: protect privacy and secrets, keep API keys on the server, enforce database access rules, check dependencies, and contain AI agents.
  • Ship projects to the public web and present them clearly.
  • Adapt when tools change: choose between chat assistants, app builders, editor agents and terminal agents, and switch to a fallback without losing work.

Each outcome is assessed through graded work scored against the rubrics in the assessment guide; for example, Explain is assessed in three oral walkthroughs (weeks 8, 12 and 14), where, with the AI off, you explain a function from your own project and find and fix a bug the examiner plants.

The assessment guide is part of this syllabus. It explains how points become your grade, the drops (your lowest two weekly scores, your lowest four in-class scores and your lowest two weekly reflections), retakes, grace tokens, late work, make-ups, pairs and regrades. I go through it in the first studio.

Attendance, absences and make-up work: how they affect your grade is set out in Absences and make-up work.

What's graded on effort, not correctness: weekly labs, timed builds such as the Game in an Hour, and exit tickets. A lab that didn't work, with a clear record of the error and what you tried, can still earn full marks.

To earn a C or higher you must also have a capstone that works at its production URL, have taken all three oral walkthroughs, and have shown each of the six skills below at least once (details).

Six skills you must be able to show without AI ​

These are checked in the oral walkthroughs, and practiced in the exit tickets (Six Skills You Must Show Without AI). Each needs to be shown only once, and you get several chances.

  1. Read an error message and find the failing line.
  2. Explain any function you committed.
  3. Write an acceptance criterion and a test for it.
  4. Revert a bad commit.
  5. Spot a committed secret.
  6. Say where authorization is enforced in your app.

Late work ​

You have two grace tokens, each extending one deadline by 48 hours with no reason needed. After that, late work loses 10% of its points per day for up to three days. Tokens can't be used for live events (studios, exit tickets, walkthroughs, the Project Fair), which have make-up rules instead. Running out of free credits is not by itself a reason to be late, because switching to a fallback is part of the course. Full rules: Late work and grace tokens.

7. AI use policy ​

This course requires AI. The rule is not "how much AI did you use" but "can you explain what you shipped". Every task is labeled with one of three levels, always in words as well as color.

LevelWhenThe complete list
🟢 AI onBuilding, explaining, learningGenerating code; asking for explanations, plans and reviews of your diffs; debugging outside the Debug Clinic; translating instructions into your own language. Everything not listed below is 🟢 AI on
🟡 AI limitedWhere the goal is your own thinkingYour SPEC.md (Project 1 and capstone): the AI may ask questions, critique it and run the Goldfish test, but you write it. Your capstone pitch: critique only. The Debug Clinic (week 7): AI off, then hint-only tutor mode after 10 minutes stuck on one bug. "You write it" (week 8): the AI may plan and review, but you write the code. Any other task a brief marks 🟡 AI limited follows that brief
🔴 AI offChecks of your understanding, and writing addressed to peopleExit tickets · oral walkthroughs, retakes, skill follow-ups and "explain it" conversations · weekly and final reflections, including the week 6 bake-off Verdict · written peer reviews (an AI review may come after yours, to compare) · peer explanation swaps · the pre- and post-course self-assessments · your personal AI-use rules (week 2, revised in finals week) · your response to the AI fluency pre-work · the first draft of your Project 1 spec (week 5) · the hand-made code changes and the feature you write by hand in week 1 · the prompt-makeover rewrites (week 4) · the micro-app's "Where the key lives and who can read it" section (week 10) · the AI-off maintenance task (week 14) · the teach-back notes and the "What I wrote or decided myself" section of PROMPTS.md · a pair's contribution statement · any other task a brief marks 🔴 AI off

If two labels ever seem to disagree, follow the stricter one and ask. Using AI on 🔴 AI-off work, or beyond what a 🟡 AI-limited label allows, is academic dishonesty under the University's Student Code of Conduct (Academic integrity).

  • Disclose: every project has a PROMPTS.md with your key prompts, links to exported chats where possible, notes on what you changed, and teach-back notes for AI-written changes. Commits made with AI help end with an Assisted-by: [tool] line.
  • Own it: "If you can't explain it, you didn't build it." You may be asked to explain any line you submit.
  • Credit others: if you use code, images or text from a person or website, credit it in your README. Using prompts you found online is fine; say where they came from.
  • Your own rules: in week 2 you write a few personal rules for when you will and won't use AI, and in finals week you revise them. Students who set their own rules tend to do better (Azimi).

8. Safety and privacy ​

The safety contract ​

Before Studio 1 you set your privacy settings and skim the safety contract; you sign it on Canvas during week 1. Each rule starts the week it's taught, and the course sets up the protections around you (a Codespace sandbox, starters that keep keys off the browser, a secret scanner). In short:

RuleIn plain wordsTaught in
1. Privacy settings firstTurn off "train on my chats" before you type anythingWeeks 0–1
2. I never paste secrets or other people's data into an AI toolNo keys, passwords, .env files or anyone else's personal information in a chatWeek 1; keys in week 9
3. I commit before and after every agent taskGit is your backup, not the agent's undoWeek 3; agents in week 11
4. I keep agents containedAgents work in a sandbox with approvals on, the smallest keys possible, hard spending caps, and no reach to your backupsWeek 9; agents in week 11
5. I verify, I don't trustCheck claims against real output; passing tests is not a security check, so test who can read and change your data yourselfWeeks 3–4 and 7; security in week 13
6. I check packages before I install themInstalling runs code: install only what the plan named, check it, and pin the versionWeek 9; again in week 14
7. I read before I adoptRead any rules file, skill, plugin, hook, MCP server or script first; never open a project folder someone sent you with an agent; clone it from its URL insteadWeek 11
8. I keep a record of what I wrote myselfPROMPTS.md, and an Assisted-by: line on AI-assisted commitsWeeks 2–3; required from week 11
9. Agents don't publish, and untrusted content never shares a session with my secretsNo agent makes anything public without your approval; an agent reading strangers' content never has your keys within reach; no autopilot modesWeek 11; prompt injection in week 14

Privacy of your work ​

Your projects are public on purpose: they become your portfolio. Your grades and personal writing are not. University policy treats grades and graded work as private (FSH 4700 F-6, and FSH 2600, which applies FERPA, the US federal student-records law), so:

  • In your public repos, under your GitHub username: code, SPEC.md, TESTS.md, PROMPTS.md, AGENTS.md, SECURITY_CHECKLIST.md and READMEs.
  • On Canvas only: reflections, exit tickets, your personal AI-use rules, the signed safety contract, self-assessments, written peer reviews, pair contribution statements, the capstone pitch, and all grades and feedback.
  • Never commit grades, feedback, reflections, classmates' real names (use GitHub usernames), your student ID, or real personal data about anyone. You don't need your real name anywhere public.

Details, including private repos and recordings: Privacy of your work.

9. Tools and cost ​

Required cost: $0. Every layer has a primary free tool and two fallbacks, listed in Tool Handout. If you choose to pay for one thing, the most useful options are the cheapest paid plan of the editor assistant, or one month of a frontier coding agent timed to the agent weeks, 11–16 (prices in TOOLS.md). Neither is needed to earn full marks.

Free tiers change without notice. When a tool stops working for you, the fallback is part of the course, not a failure: see Troubleshooting: I ran out of free credits. Never add a payment method to a free account just to keep going; ask first.

10. If you fall behind ​

Each week builds on the last, so act early:

  • Do the Minimum to keep up box at the end of each week's homework first.
  • If Project 1 isn't working by week 7, a rescue starter (a small working app built from the course's example spec) lets you keep going: projects/rescue-starters/.
  • Use the studios' project-work time and office hours. "I don't understand what the AI did" is exactly what office hours are for.
