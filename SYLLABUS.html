3 credits

[Semester and year, as scheduled]

  • Name: [name]
  • Email: [email]
  • Phone: [phone]

Email response time: normally within 24 hours on weekdays, possibly longer over weekends and holidays.

Prerequisites ​

One programming course in any language, such as CS 1112, CS 1120, CS 2120 or another department's programming course; equivalent programming experience; or instructor permission. No experience with web development, the terminal or git is needed, and From Python to JavaScript covers what changes for students who know Python. CS students who have completed CS 3383 Software Engineering should take Engineering Software with AI (CS [4xxx]/[5xxx]) instead.

Course Description ​

This course teaches students from any major to build and ship web apps by directing AI tools, and to apply the engineering habits that make AI-written software trustworthy. Topics include how large language models write code; HTML, CSS and JavaScript; prompting; version control with git and GitHub; specifications with testable acceptance criteria; automated testing; reading and debugging code; APIs, servers and secret keys; AI agents and code review through pull requests; database access rules; web security; and deployment. Students learn by building, in two hands-on studios a week. They begin with a chat assistant and finish with an AI agent working in their own repository, shipping a series of projects to the public web with free tools and ending with a capstone of their own choosing. The course introduces vibe coding and its limits, and it emphasizes writing the specification before the prompt, checking AI output, protecting secrets and data, and being able to explain every line of code without AI.

Required Textbooks and Materials ​

No textbook. Students need a laptop with a modern browser and several free accounts; the required cost is $0 (Tool Handout lists every tool and its free fallbacks). Before the first class, students complete the first-hour quick start, which takes 60 to 90 minutes.

Learning Outcomes ​

At the end of this course, students will be able to:

  • Build working web software, from single-file pages to a full-stack app, by directing chat assistants, app builders and coding agents through a repeatable workflow (the Safe Loop), and switch to a fallback tool without losing work.
  • Develop a one-page specification with user stories and testable acceptance criteria before building.
  • Evaluate AI output rather than trust it: write the check first, test normal and edge cases, and read the diff.
  • Explain every part of the code they submit, and debug a failing program by reading its error messages, without AI.
  • Apply version control with git and GitHub as a safety net, committing, comparing, reverting, branching and reviewing changes through pull requests.
  • Deploy projects to the public web and present them clearly while protecting privacy and secrets, which includes keeping API keys on the server, enforcing database access rules, checking dependencies and containing AI agents.

Course Outline ​

The course follows a standard 16-week semester. Weeks 1–15 have two studios a week; week 16 is finals week, when the Project Fair takes place in the scheduled two-hour final exam slot.

Evaluation Procedures ​

Assessment ​

Grades are based on evidence of process and understanding as well as on whether a program runs. Weekly labs, timed builds and exit tickets are graded on effort and completion rather than correctness.

ComponentWeight
Weekly labs and homework20%
In-class builds, exit tickets and participation15%
Project 110%
Capstone30%
Oral walkthroughs (weeks 8, 12, 14)15%
AI-free reflections and peer reviews10%

The assessment guide is part of this syllabus. It contains the rubrics, the conversion of points to a grade, the dropped scores and retakes, and the rules on attendance, absences and make-up work. Each of the six skills students must show without AI must be shown at least once, in an oral walkthrough, a retake or a short follow-up.

Final Examination ​

There is no written final examination. In week 16, the Project Fair takes place in the course's scheduled two-hour final exam slot and is scored like a studio. Students present their capstones in a gallery walk, try one another's apps and leave feedback, and complete the post-course self-assessment without AI. The capstone's final product (the production URL with a screenshot, the v1.0 release and the demo video) is due at the start of the Fair. The final reflection and the revised personal AI-use rules are due at 11:59 pm on the last day of finals week.

Performance Standards and Grading Policy ​

Letter grades are A (90–100%), B (80–89%), C (70–79%), D (60–69%) and F (below 60%). A grade of C or higher also requires a capstone that works at its production URL, all three oral walkthroughs, and each of the six skills shown without AI at least once (details).

Assignments ​

  • Weekly labs and homework (weeks 1–15), including the home page and its game in weeks 1–4 and the AI micro-app with the capstone pitch in weeks 9–10. Homework is due before the next week's Studio A unless Canvas states otherwise. Students who fall behind should complete each week's "Minimum to keep up" section first.
  • In-class work and exit tickets: lab work, peer explanation and checkpoints in every studio, and a short exit ticket, written without AI, in weeks 1–14.
  • Projects: Project 1, A Tool I'd Actually Use (weeks 5–8), and the capstone (weeks 11–16), a deployed full-stack app of the student's choice built with an AI agent, individually or in a pair. Every project repository keeps a PROMPTS.md log of how AI was used. Briefs, project rules and submission steps are in the projects guide. A student whose Project 1 is not working by week 7 may continue from a rescue starter.
  • Oral walkthroughs in weeks 8, 12 and 14: ten-minute conversations without AI in which students explain their code, change it live and find a planted bug (protocol).
  • Reflections and peer reviews: weekly reflections, two written peer reviews and a final reflection, all written without AI.

Graded personal writing, including reflections, peer reviews, exit tickets, self-assessments and personal AI-use rules, is submitted on Canvas, the course's learning management system, and never placed in a repository (privacy of your work).

Late Work Policy ​

Each student has two grace tokens; each extends one deadline by 48 hours, with no reason required. Beyond that, late work loses 10% of its points per day for up to three days and then scores zero unless an extension has been agreed. Tokens do not apply to live events (studios, exit tickets, oral walkthroughs and the Project Fair), which have their own make-up rules. Running out of free AI credits does not by itself justify a late submission, because switching to a fallback tool is part of the course. The full rules are under late work and grace tokens.

AI Use ​

AI tools are a required part of this course. Grades depend on whether students can explain the work they submit; the amount of AI they used does not matter. Every task carries one of three labels: AI on, AI limited or AI off. Exit tickets, oral walkthroughs, reflections, written peer reviews, self-assessments and personal AI-use rules are always AI off. Students are responsible for understanding, validating and explaining all work they submit, and may be asked to explain any line of it. AI help is disclosed in each project's PROMPTS.md log and in an Assisted-by: [tool] line on AI-assisted commits. The complete lists of AI-limited and AI-off work are in the AI use policy, and the rules for working safely with AI tools are in the safety contract.

Academic Honesty ​

Academic honesty is important at the University of Idaho because it is the way you improve over time. Academic dishonesty, which includes cheating and plagiarism, is not tolerated at the University of Idaho.

Plagiarism means claiming another's work as your own. That can happen in several ways:

  • Copying another's work in whole or in part
  • Quoting another's work without attribution
  • Paraphrasing another's work without attribution
  • Purchasing another's work from "study aid" websites

In this course it also includes using AI where a task is marked AI off, and hiding AI help that the AI Use rules ask you to disclose. Suspected academic dishonesty is handled under the University's Student Code of Conduct and Resolution Process (FSH 2300), which includes reporting it to the Dean of Students. If you have any questions about how to cite a source or credit an AI tool, please ask me.

University of Idaho Classroom Learning Civility Clause ​

In any environment in which people gather to learn, it is essential that all members feel as free and safe as possible in their participation. To this end, it is expected that everyone in this course will be treated with mutual respect and civility, with an understanding that all of us (students, instructors, professors, guests, and teaching assistants) will be respectful and civil to one another in discussion, in action, in teaching, and in learning.

Should you feel our classroom interactions do not reflect an environment of civility and respect, you are encouraged to meet with your instructor during office hours to discuss your concern. Additional resources for expression of concern or requesting support include the Dean of Students office and staff, the UI Counseling and Mental Health Center, or the UI Office of Civil Rights and Investigations.

Firearms ​

The University of Idaho bans firearms from its property with only limited exceptions. One exception applies to persons who hold a valid Idaho enhanced concealed carry license, provided those firearms remain concealed at all times. If an enhanced concealed carry license holder's firearm is displayed, other than in necessary self-defense, it is a violation of University policy. Please contact local law enforcement (call 911) to report firearms on University property. University of Idaho leadership remains committed to maintaining a safe work, living and learning environment on campus. We will not tolerate any threatening use of firearms or any other weapons. While authorized license holders may have familiarity and be at ease carrying a loaded firearm, we ask that they be aware that many people are not familiar with handguns and are uncomfortable in their presence.
