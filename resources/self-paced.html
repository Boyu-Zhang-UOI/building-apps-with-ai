Self-Paced Path ​

How to do Building Apps with AI without a class: on your own from the public materials, as the only student in a section, or catching up on a studio you missed. Everything in the course is written to work without an instructor. This page says what replaces each studio activity, what to do instead of every pair activity, and how to check yourself.

On this page: Who this is for · Time · Each week · What replaces the studio · Working alone? · Tutor mode · Checking yourself · Your private writing · Falling behind

Who this is for ​

  • Learning on your own from the public course. You have no instructor, no Canvas and no grades, but you can do every lab, homework and project. The rules about AI use, safety and privacy still apply, because they protect you. Start with the safety contract and the week 0 setup, and check the age and region notes in Tool Handout: some free tools are 18+ or not available everywhere.
  • Enrolled, but the only student (or one of very few). Your instructor posts the substitutions for your course on Canvas in week 1, such as a practice spec and a practice pull request with known issues to find. This page tells you what each one replaces. Where a line below says Enrolled, follow it; where it says On your own, that's for independent learners.
  • Enrolled, and you missed a studio. Do the lab's Core parts on your own using this page, then follow the make-up rules in the assessment guide. Online or in another time zone for part of the course? Talk to your instructor in week 1 (assessment guide).

What you need first. The course assumes you've taken one programming course in any language (often Python), or have the same experience another way: you can write a loop, a condition and a function. It doesn't assume you know HTML, CSS, JavaScript, the terminal, git or how the web works; the course teaches those. If you know Python but not JavaScript, work through From Python to JavaScript in week 0 (about 60–90 minutes).

Using a screen reader or only a keyboard? See the accessibility guide first.

How much time ​

The course is built for about 8.5 hours a week for 16 weeks: two 75-minute studios plus about 6 hours outside class. Weeks 1–15 teach; week 16 is the Project Fair and the final submission. On your own, the studio time becomes lab time, so the total is about the same (about 135 hours). Two common ways to fit it in:

PaceHours a weekNotes
16 weeksabout 8–9The course as designed: one course week per calendar week. Do Studio A's lab early in the week and Studio B's a few days later, so you practice each skill twice
8 weeksabout 15–17Two course weeks per calendar week, paired 1+2, 3+4, … 15+16 (the course's accelerated version). Do both Studio A labs, then both Studio B labs, and never split a timed activity

The first time you use any tool takes longer than the stated times. Going slower is fine. Skipping the AI-off checks is not: they're how you find out what you actually learned.

Each week, in order ​

  1. Read the week's README.md, starting with This week at a glance. It lists what's due, the AI rules for the week, and the Minimum to keep up.
  2. Before Studio A: the README and at most one short reading (about 30 minutes).
  3. Studio A: go through slides.md (the concept talk), then the Studio A section of lab.md. Do the Core parts first; If time parts are optional.
  4. Studio B: the Studio B section of lab.md.
  5. Instead of the exit ticket (weeks 1–14): answer the README's Understanding check with the AI off, before looking anything up. Then, from memory, explain one function or decision from the project you worked on two weeks earlier in two or three sentences, and check yourself against the code. Weeks 15 and 16 have no exit ticket.
  6. Homework: the core tier of homework.md. Do the Minimum to keep up box first.
  7. Reflection: write it with the AI off, using the reflection template. Keep it private (where).

Every lab uses the Safe Loop. Keep it next to your keyboard.

Using tutor mode ​

Tutor mode is an AI that gives hints and asks questions instead of handing you answers. Working alone, it's the nearest thing to a TA. It's set up by pasting one prompt into a fresh chat.

When to use it:

  • 🟡 AI limited: in the Debug Clinic, after 10 minutes stuck on one bug. It's the only AI allowed there.
  • When you want to learn something, not just get it done, in any homework.
  • To practice for an oral walkthrough: the "quiz me" prompt asks you questions about your own code.

How:

  1. Open a new chat, a temporary one if your assistant offers it.
  2. Paste the tutor prompt from the tutor mode page. (Gemini users can save it as a Gem: how.)
  3. Describe your problem: what you're working on, the exact error text, and what you think is wrong. End with "Give me a hint, not the answer." An example is in prompt pattern 14.
  4. If it hands you the answer, paste the prompt again or start a fresh chat. If a hint doesn't help, ask for a smaller one.

For walkthrough practice, use the quiz-me prompt on a function you committed.

Keep it narrow. Use tutor mode for being stuck and for practice, not as a companion for every step. In a 2026 trial with 2,379 students, giving every student a course-wide AI tutor lowered final grades, most of all for first-generation students; the authors think it pulled students away from the course's own activities (EdWorkingPapers). Do the lab, then ask for a hint. More on why: tutor mode.

Checking yourself: the six skills ​

The course asks you to show six skills with the AI off. Without an examiner, check them this way:

  1. Practice each skill with the exercises on Six Skills You Must Show Without AI, answers hidden until you've tried.
  2. Record a walkthrough in weeks 8, 12 and 14: AI off (close the chat and switch off code suggestions: how), screen and voice recording on, about 10 minutes. Use the questions for that walkthrough in Oral Walkthroughs, picking them at random so you can't rehearse the answer. Read what to expect first.
  3. Watch it back and mark each skill on the list as shown (done correctly, with no more than one look at your notes) or not yet.
  4. Better still, find a questioner: a friend reading the questions aloud makes it much closer to the real thing, even if they don't code.

A self-check is weaker than an examiner, so be strict with yourself. Any skill that's still not yet is next week's practice.

Your private writing ​

Your code and project documents (SPEC.md, TESTS.md, PROMPTS.md, AGENTS.md, READMEs) belong in public repositories under your GitHub username: they're your portfolio. Your personal writing does not: reflections, your AI journal, your personal AI-use rules, self-assessments and reviews of other people's work.

  • Enrolled: submit it on Canvas, as everyone does (privacy of your work).
  • On your own: keep it in a private document or notebook. Never commit it to a public repository, and never name other people in your repos.

If you fall behind ​

  • Do each week's Minimum to keep up box first. Every week builds on the one before.
  • Project 1 not working by week 7? A rescue starter, a small working app built from the course's example spec, lets you keep going: projects/rescue-starters/study-timer/.
  • Out of free credits? Switch to the fallback (how). That's a planned drill, not a failure.
  • Stuck for real? Write a good bug report. Writing it often shows you the answer; if not, it's exactly what to bring to office hours if you're enrolled, or to share with someone who can help if you're not.
  • Answer keys for the course's exercises exist for instructors. Looking at them before you've tried turns an exercise into reading. Try first.
